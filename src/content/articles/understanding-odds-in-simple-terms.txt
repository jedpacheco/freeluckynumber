Odds come up all the time: weather forecasts, sports, raffles, board games and lottery tickets. But the numbers can be confusing, and it's easy to get a wrong feeling about how likely something really is. Here's a simple guide.

## Chance as "1 in something"

The simplest way to describe chance is to count. If there's one winning ticket in a raffle of 100 tickets and you hold one, your chance of winning is 1 in 100. As a percentage, that's 1 percent. The formula is: winning outcomes divided by total outcomes.

A few everyday examples:

- Flipping heads on a fair coin: 1 in 2, or 50 percent.
- Rolling a 6 on a regular die: 1 in 6, or about 16.7 percent.
- Drawing an ace from a full deck of cards: 4 in 52, or about 7.7 percent.

## Odds versus probability

In everyday speech, people use "odds" and "probability" as if they're the same, but there's a small difference. Probability compares winning outcomes to all outcomes, so rolling a 6 is 1 in 6. Odds compare winning outcomes to losing outcomes, so the odds of rolling a 6 are 1 to 5. Lotteries usually publish "1 in" figures, which are probabilities.

## Why very small chances are hard to picture

Our brains handle numbers like 1 in 10 pretty well. Numbers like 1 in 292 million, which is roughly the chance of winning the Powerball jackpot with one ticket, are almost impossible to imagine. One way to picture it: if you picked one second at random out of more than nine years, that's about how likely it is.

That's why seeing odds visually helps. Our [Simple Odds Calculator](/odds) shows any chance as a percentage and a meter, so you can see how tiny some of them really are.

## Past results don't change the next one

For independent events like coin flips, dice and lottery draws, what happened before has no effect on what happens next. A coin that landed on heads five times in a row still has a 50 percent chance of heads on the next flip. Believing otherwise is called [the gambler's fallacy](/articles/gamblers-fallacy-explained), and it's behind a lot of [lottery myths](/articles/lottery-myths-hot-and-cold-numbers).

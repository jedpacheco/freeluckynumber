Ask a room full of people to name a lucky number and you'll hear "seven" more than anything else. It's on slot machines, in fairy tales and in more song titles than anyone could count. In 2014 the math writer Alex Bellos asked people online for their favourite number. He reported in The Guardian that 44,000 people took part and that almost 4,000 of them chose 7, which put 7 first. He also wrote that the survey was self-selecting, so it is not a laboratory experiment. It says what that group of volunteers liked. It does not show that 7 changes what happens to someone. [Alex Bellos, The Guardian, 12 April 2014](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2014/apr/13/favourite-number-survey-psychology).

So why 7? There isn't one neat answer. It's more like a pile of reasons that have been building up for thousands of years, from the sky, from religion, from how our brains handle information and from plain old repetition. Let's go through the main ones, and keep tradition separate from the counts you can check.

## Seven things in the sky

Long before telescopes, people looking up at night could see seven objects that moved across the sky differently from the stars: the Sun, the Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. A lunar cycle is about 29.5 days, so each quarter is a little over seven days. That is an observation about the sky, not a claim that the number is lucky.

Historians do not tell this as one single origin story. Kristin Heineman, a history instructor at Colorado State University, wrote for The Conversation that the seven-day week we inherited is in large part Babylonian, and that those seven celestial bodies are why seven mattered there. [Why are there seven days in a week?](https://source.colostate.edu/why-are-there-seven-days-in-a-week/) Other cultures used weeks of a different length, which she also notes.

You can check the day names without a history book. In English, Sunday, Monday and Saturday still point at the Sun, the Moon and Saturn. Spanish martes, miércoles, jueves and viernes come from Mars, Mercury, Jupiter and Venus. Lowell Observatory walks through those names, including the Norse gods that sit on top of the Roman ones in English. [The planets and their ties to the days of the week](https://lowell.edu/view-from-mars-hill-the-planets-and-their-ties-to-the-days-of-the-week/). The names are a record of an old sky story. They are not evidence that a day named for a planet is a better day to take a chance.

## Seven in religion and stories

Seven shows up all over religious texts. In the Bible, the world is created in six days and the seventh is a day of rest. There are seven deadly sins, seven virtues and seven seals in the book of Revelation. In Islam, pilgrims on the Hajj walk around the Kaaba seven times, and there are said to be seven heavens. Hinduism describes seven chakras in popular modern teachings and seven steps in a traditional wedding ceremony called saptapadi.

Folklore loves it too. There are seven dwarfs, seven seas, seven wonders of the ancient world and seven-league boots. Once a number becomes a storytelling habit, each new story makes it feel even more special.

## Why our brains might like 7

There's also a psychological angle, and it is easy to overstate. In 1956 George A. Miller published "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two" in Psychological Review. He gathered results in which people could hold about seven chunks on certain memory tasks. He also refused to treat every cultural seven as one deep law. The paper ends by suspecting that the pile of sevens is a coincidence. [Miller, Psychological Review, 1956, volume 63, pages 81–97](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043158).

Nelson Cowan later argued that when experiments stop people from bundling items into bigger chunks, a central short-term limit averages about four chunks, often put at three to five. He described Miller's seven as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device, not a precise constant. [Cowan, Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2001, volume 24, pages 87–114](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922). A readable copy is on [Cowan's site](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf). Memory research does not make 7 a lucky number.

A different study is often mixed up with both of those papers. Michael Kubovy and Joseph Psotka asked 558 people for the first number between 0 and 9 that came to mind. 28.4 percent said 7, more than any other digit in that range. Their later experiments pointed to a deliberate choice: people picked a number that would look spontaneous. The prompt was 0 to 9, not 1 to 10, and 7 was a plurality, not a majority. [Kubovy and Psotka, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 1976, volume 2, pages 291–294](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.2.2.291). Bellos, in the Guardian article above, gives his own arithmetic reason for why 7 feels distinctive among 1 to 10: it is not even, not an endpoint and not the middle value 5. That is his reading of the pattern, not a second laboratory result.

## Lucky 7 in games

A row of 7s is a familiar jackpot symbol on slot machines. That is a design choice by the people who made the machines. It does not make the digit 7 more likely in a lottery draw, and this article does not treat a particular year or machine as the start of the symbol.

Dice are a case where 7 really is the most common total, and the reason is counting. Two six-sided dice have 6 × 6 = 36 ordered rolls. The totals of 7 are 1+6, 6+1, 2+5, 5+2, 3+4 and 4+3. That is 6 rolls out of 36, or 1 in 6. A total of 2 or 12 can happen in only 1 way. In craps, a 7 on the first roll wins, which is a rule of that game sitting on top of this count. The count does not transfer to a lottery. A lottery does not add two dice. You can load these 6 winning rolls and 36 possible rolls in the [Simple Odds Calculator](/odds).

## Seven around the world

Seven isn't lucky everywhere, which is part of what makes number superstitions fun. In Japan, 7 is generally considered lucky, and there's a group of folk deities called the Seven Lucky Gods. In China, 7 is more mixed. The seventh month of the lunar calendar is known as Ghost Month, when some people believe spirits roam, so it's seen as a bad time to move house or get married. Meanwhile 8 is the big lucky number there. We cover that in more detail in [lucky and unlucky numbers around the world](/articles/lucky-and-unlucky-numbers-around-the-world).

## Is 7 actually lucky?

In the sense of making good things happen, no. A 7 on a lottery ticket has exactly the same chance of being drawn as any other number. A coin doesn't know what day of the week it is. And a house number 7 won't bring better fortune than number 12.

But luck isn't only about outcomes. Having a favorite number can be part of who you are. Athletes pick jersey number 7 because of players they grew up watching. Couples get married on July 7. People set their alarms for 7:07. It's a small ritual that makes a day feel a bit more personal, and that's a perfectly good reason to like it.

What we'd suggest avoiding is the idea that picking 7 gives you an edge in a game of chance. It doesn't, and if lots of other people pick it too, you'd actually have to share any prize with more of them. We explain that idea in [birthday numbers and the lottery](/articles/birthday-numbers-and-the-lottery).

## Seven in numerology

In numerology, 7 is seen as the number of the thinker and the seeker. People with a 7 [life path number](/articles/what-your-life-path-number-says-about-you) are described as curious, private and drawn to big questions. As with all numerology, that's a tradition, not a scientific finding, but it fits the mysterious reputation 7 has picked up over the centuries.

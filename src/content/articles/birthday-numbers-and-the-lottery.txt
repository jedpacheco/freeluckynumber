Ask people how they pick their lottery numbers and a lot of them will say the same thing: birthdays. Their own, their kids', their partner's, their mom's. It's one of the most popular methods in the world, and it's easy to see why. Those dates mean something, they're easy to remember and they feel lucky.

There's a catch, though, and it's not the one you might expect. Birthday numbers don't lower your odds of winning. What they can do is change how much you'd win. Let's look at why.

## The 1 to 31 problem

Calendar dates only go up to 31. Months only go up to 12. So if you build a ticket from birthdays, every number you pick will be 31 or lower.

But most big lottery games use far more numbers than that. In Powerball, you pick five main numbers from 1 to 69. In Mega Millions, it's five from 1 to 70. Florida Lotto uses 1 to 53. That means more than half of the numbers in those games can never come from a birthday.

When millions of people pick birthday numbers, the low numbers, 1 to 31, end up on far more tickets than the high numbers. And dates in the first half of the month, plus 1 to 12 from months, get even more love.

## Does that change your chance of winning?

No. This part is important. In a fair draw, every combination has exactly the same chance of coming up. The balls don't know which numbers people like. A ticket with 3, 8, 14, 22 and 27 has the same chance as a ticket with 41, 52, 58, 63 and 67.

So you're not hurting your odds by playing birthdays. You're also not helping them.

## Where it matters: sharing the prize

Here's the real effect. When more than one ticket matches the jackpot, the prize is split between them. And when you play numbers that lots of other people also play, you're more likely to end up sharing if you win.

Think about what happens when the winning numbers are all 31 or lower. That draw is a perfect match for all the "birthday" tickets out there. More people tend to win, and each one gets a smaller slice. When the winning numbers include several high numbers, fewer people have them, and a winner is more likely to keep the whole thing.

Researchers have looked at this in real lottery data. Studies of games in the UK, Canada and elsewhere have found that low numbers and dates are overrepresented on tickets, and draws with mostly low numbers tend to have more winners. One often-cited example is a UK National Lottery draw in January 1995, when 133 people shared the jackpot. That's far more winners than you'd expect by chance, and it's been pointed to ever since as a sign of how many people pick the same popular numbers.

## Other popular picks

Birthdays aren't the only crowded choice. People also tend to:

- Play lucky 7 and multiples of 7. See [why 7 is called the luckiest number](/articles/why-7-is-the-luckiest-number) for the reasons.
- Pick straight lines or diagonal patterns on the play slip.
- Use sequences like 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.
- Play numbers that appeared on TV, in movies or in viral posts.
- Repeat the last winning combination.

None of these affect your chance of winning. They just make it more likely that other people have the same numbers.

## Should you stop playing birthdays?

That's up to you. If birthdays are part of what makes playing fun for you, keep playing them. For a lot of people, the meaning of the numbers is the whole point. A grandmother playing her grandkids' birthdays every week isn't doing it for the math.

If you'd rather reduce the chance of splitting a prize, a few approaches help:

- Include some numbers above 31.
- Use a random generator or a quick pick, which spreads numbers evenly across the whole range. Our [Lottery Number Generator](/tools/lottery-number-generator) does this.
- Avoid obvious patterns on the slip.

## Put the odds in perspective

Whatever numbers you choose, the odds of winning a big jackpot are extremely small. For Powerball, it's about 1 in 292.2 million per ticket. For Mega Millions, about 1 in 290.5 million. Even Florida Lotto is about 1 in 23 million. Worrying about whether you'd split the prize is a bit like worrying about where you'd park your spaceship. It's fun to think about, but it's very unlikely to come up.

That's why we encourage people to treat lottery tickets as entertainment. Spend what you'd happily spend on a movie or a snack, and enjoy the daydream. Never spend money you need for rent, bills or groceries, and only play if you're of legal age where you live.

Numbers mean different things depending on where you grew up. A number that gets a cheer at a wedding in one country might make people uncomfortable in another. Some of these beliefs are so strong that they shape phone numbers, house prices, building design and even the dates of huge public events.

Most of these superstitions have a simple root: the way a number sounds in the local language, or a story that got told so many times it turned into tradition. Here's a tour of some of the best-known ones.

## China: 8 is great, 4 is not

In Mandarin, the word for eight is "bā," which sounds close to "fā," part of the word for getting rich or prospering. That one sound has made 8 the luckiest number in Chinese culture. The opening ceremony of the 2008 Beijing Olympics started on August 8, 2008, at 8:08 in the evening, and that timing was very much on purpose. Phone numbers and license plates full of 8s can sell for huge sums at auction.

Four is the opposite. "Sì" sounds a lot like "sǐ," the word for death. Many buildings in China, Hong Kong and Taiwan skip the fourth floor, or any floor with a 4 in it. Some apartment buildings jump from 3 to 5, and some skip 14, 24 and so on. Hospitals are especially careful about this.

Other numbers have their own meanings. Six, "liù," sounds like the word for flowing smoothly, so 66 or 666 can mean things are going well. That's very different from how 666 is seen in parts of the Christian world. Nine, "jiǔ," sounds like the word for long-lasting, which makes it popular for weddings and anniversaries.

## Japan: 4 and 9

Japan shares the worry about 4. One way to say four in Japanese is "shi," which is also a word for death. Nine can be read as "ku," which sounds like the word for suffering or pain. Because of this, some hospitals and hotels avoid room numbers with 4 or 9, and gifts are rarely given in sets of four.

On the lucky side, Japan has a soft spot for 7 and 8. The Seven Lucky Gods are a well-known group in Japanese folklore, and 8 is considered lucky because the written character widens toward the bottom, which suggests growth and a widening future.

## Korea: the missing fourth floor

South Korea has the same sound problem with four. "Sa" sounds like the word for death, and many elevators label the fourth floor "F" instead of 4. You'll see this in apartment buildings and hospitals across the country.

## Italy: 17, and 13 is fine

In Italy, the number people worry about is 17. Written in Roman numerals, 17 is XVII. If you rearrange those letters, you get VIXI, a Latin word that means "I have lived," which was found on ancient tombstones. In other words, "my life is over." Some Italian airplanes have skipped row 17, and Friday the 17th, not the 13th, is the classic unlucky day.

Thirteen, on the other hand, isn't really a problem in Italy. It's even considered lucky in some parts of the country. In Italian slang, "fare tredici," or "to make thirteen," means to hit the jackpot, a phrase that comes from an old football betting game with 13 matches to predict.

## The English-speaking world and Europe: 13

In the United States, the UK and much of Europe, 13 is the classic unlucky number. Many tall buildings in the U.S. skip the 13th floor, some airlines skip row 13 and Friday the 13th gets its own movies. The fear even has a name: triskaidekaphobia. The history is longer than you might think, and we cover it in [why is 13 unlucky](/articles/why-is-13-unlucky).

Meanwhile, 7 is the go-to lucky number across most English-speaking countries. Our article on [why 7 is called the luckiest number](/articles/why-7-is-the-luckiest-number) explains why.

## Spain and Latin America: Tuesday the 13th

In Spain and most of Latin America, the unlucky day isn't Friday the 13th. It's Tuesday the 13th, "martes trece." There's a well-known saying: "En martes, ni te cases ni te embarques," which means "On Tuesday, don't get married or set sail." Tuesday, martes, takes its name from Mars, the Roman god of war, which may explain the bad reputation.

## Other examples

- In Afghanistan, 39 has had a strong stigma for years because of a rumor linking it to a slang term. People have reportedly avoided license plates and phone numbers containing it.
- In Thailand, 9 is considered lucky because the word "kâo" sounds like a word for moving forward. Many Thai people choose dates with 9 for important events.
- In Hindu traditions, odd numbers are often preferred for gifts of money, which is why you'll see amounts like 101 or 501 rupees in wedding envelopes.
- In parts of Europe, giving an even number of flowers is linked with funerals, so bouquets for happy occasions usually have an odd number of stems.

## Why these beliefs stick

Psychologists have a few explanations for why number superstitions last so long. One is confirmation bias: when something bad happens on a day with an unlucky number, we notice it and remember it, and we forget the many ordinary days. Another is social learning. If you grew up in a building without a fourth floor, avoiding 4 just feels normal.

There's also a practical side. Even people who don't believe in it often go along with it out of respect, or because they know it'll matter to someone else. A hotel owner may not care about 13 at all but still skip it because some guests do.

## Are some numbers really luckier than others?

In terms of outcomes, no. A dice roll, a card draw or a lottery machine has no idea what a number means in Mandarin or Italian. Each possible result has the same odds no matter what culture you're in. We explain how that works in [the gambler's fallacy explained](/articles/gamblers-fallacy-explained).

But meaning isn't the same as probability. Lucky numbers carry real cultural weight. They're part of weddings, birthdays, family stories and holiday traditions. You don't have to believe a number will change your luck to enjoy using one that matters to you.

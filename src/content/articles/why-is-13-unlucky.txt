Walk into a lot of tall buildings in the United States and press the elevator buttons. There's a good chance the numbers jump from 12 to 14. Of course there's still a 13th floor. It's just been renamed, because enough people would rather not live or work on it. Some airlines skip row 13, some hotels skip room 13, and plenty of people get a little uneasy when the 13th of the month lands on a Friday.

So where did this come from? Most people assume there's one clear origin. There isn't. The fear of 13 seems to have grown from several stories that got mixed together over time, and some of the most popular explanations don't hold up very well. Let's go through them.

## The fear has a name

The fear of the number 13 is called triskaidekaphobia, from the Greek words for three, ten and fear. The fear of Friday the 13th has its own name too: paraskevidekatriaphobia. Both words were coined in the 20th century, which tells you something. The superstition is old, but it became much more widespread in modern times.

## Twelve is a "complete" number

One explanation is that 13 suffers by comparison with 12. Twelve shows up as a number of completeness in many cultures. There are 12 months in a year, 12 signs of the zodiac, 12 hours on a clock face, 12 tribes of Israel, 12 apostles and 12 Olympian gods in Greek myth. Twelve divides neatly into halves, thirds and quarters, which made it handy for counting and trade.

Thirteen comes right after that. It's a prime number that doesn't divide into anything, and it breaks the tidy set. Some historians think that alone made it feel odd or out of place.

## The thirteenth guest

Two famous stories involve a 13th person at a table.

In Christian tradition, there were 13 people at the Last Supper: Jesus and his twelve apostles. Judas, who betrayed Jesus, is sometimes described as the 13th to arrive. Over time, this turned into a belief that having 13 people at a dinner table is bad luck. In 19th-century Europe, some hosts would invite an extra guest or seat a doll at the table to avoid it. In Paris, people could even hire a professional "fourteenth guest," called a quatorzième, for a dinner party.

In Norse mythology, there's a story about a feast of twelve gods in Valhalla. The trickster god Loki shows up uninvited as the 13th guest and arranges the death of Baldur, one of the most beloved gods. It's often cited as proof that 13 was unlucky in pre-Christian Europe, though historians point out that the versions we know were written down centuries later, after Christianity had arrived in Scandinavia.

## Friday the 13th

Friday has its own bad reputation in Christian tradition, since the crucifixion is said to have happened on a Friday. In old English sayings, Friday was seen as a poor day to start a journey or a new project.

The combination of Friday and 13 seems to be fairly recent. Historians generally can't find much written evidence of Friday the 13th as a specifically unlucky day before the late 1800s. A 1907 novel by Thomas W. Lawson called "Friday, the Thirteenth," about a stockbroker who uses superstition to crash the market, helped make the idea popular.

You may have heard that it all started on Friday, October 13, 1307, when King Philip IV of France had members of the Knights Templar arrested. That arrest really did happen on that date, but there's no evidence that people connected it to the superstition until much later. It's a good story that was added on after the fact.

## The Code of Hammurabi myth

Here's a myth that gets repeated a lot. People say the ancient Code of Hammurabi, a set of Babylonian laws from around 1750 BC, skips law number 13 because the number was unlucky. It's true that some translations don't have a 13th law. But that's because the numbering was added by modern scholars, and one section of the original stone was damaged or unclear. It wasn't an ancient superstition at all.

## Thirteen in modern life

The superstition is strong enough to affect real decisions. Some estimates have suggested that most high-rise buildings in the U.S. don't label a 13th floor. Formula One stopped using car number 13 for many years. Some people refuse to schedule surgery or travel on Friday the 13th.

Then there's Apollo 13, the NASA mission launched in April 1970 at 13:13 Houston time. An oxygen tank exploded on April 13, and the crew had to abort the moon landing. They made it home safely, which is why some people see it as a lucky story rather than an unlucky one. It's a good example of how we notice the number when something goes wrong and forget it when nothing happens.

## Is 13 actually unlucky?

Researchers have looked. A 1993 study in the British Medical Journal compared traffic accidents on Friday the 13th and Friday the 6th in one part of England and suggested the 13th might be riskier. The authors themselves said it was partly tongue in cheek, and the sample was tiny. Larger studies since then, including research by a Dutch insurance group, have found no meaningful increase in accidents on Friday the 13th. If anything, some found fewer, possibly because nervous people stay home or drive more carefully.

In games of chance, the number 13 has the same odds as any other number. A roulette wheel doesn't avoid it, and lottery machines don't treat it differently. If you'd like to know why "unlucky" and "lucky" numbers don't change your chances, read [the gambler's fallacy explained](/articles/gamblers-fallacy-explained).

## Not everyone fears 13

Thirteen isn't unlucky everywhere. In Italy, it's often seen as lucky, and the unlucky number is 17. In Spain and much of Latin America, the unlucky day is Tuesday the 13th, not Friday. Some sports players, including several famous basketball and football stars, wear 13 on purpose.
